# Momentum Rules

*Starting doesn't require motivation. Motivation shows up after you start — these rules are just about making the "start" as small and automatic as possible.*

---

## 1. Start before you feel ready

Readiness doesn't come first. It's not the thing that lets you begin — it's what begins once you've already started. Waiting to feel like it is waiting for a signal that usually only arrives *after* you're moving.

**Examples:**
- Don't wait to "feel like studying" — open the document. The feeling of it being manageable shows up a few minutes in, not before.
- Don't wait to want to reply to a message you've been avoiding — open the chat and type one line.
- Don't wait for the urge to clean — pick up one item. The urge to keep going tends to follow.

---

## 2. Shrink it until starting is trivial

The task is never "do the whole thing." It's the smallest possible first move — small enough that skipping it would feel silly.

**Examples:**
- Not "cook dinner" — just "pick up the knife."
- Not "go to the gym" — just "put the bag by the door."
- Not "finish the report" — just "open the file and write one sentence."

---

## 3. Give it a built-in stop

Open-ended tasks feel heavier because part of you is calculating "how long is this going to go on" before you've even started. Physical tasks often have a natural stop already (the chicken's done when it's cut) — trust those. For anything open-ended, decide the stop *before* you start.

**Examples:**
- Studying → one timer (e.g. one Pomodoro). When it rings, stopping is allowed. No guilt.
- A workout → a preset plan with defined exercises, not "however long feels right." The plan itself is the stop.
- Laundry → "one load," not "get the laundry situation sorted."

---

## 4. The bar is "did it happen," not "was it good"

Grading the quality is what makes starting feel expensive. On a low-capacity day, showing up at all is the entire win. A short, messy session is still real progress.

**Examples:**
- A 10-minute study session that felt scattered still counts — it happened.
- A workout done at low intensity is still a workout done.
- A one-line reply is still a reply. It doesn't need to be the perfect message.

---

## 5. Lead with the physical one

Physical tasks give you something to *see* change — proof of movement, even on days when you can't feel that anything moved. Abstract tasks (studying, admin, planning) don't offer that same visible proof, which is part of why they feel heavier to start. Do the physical thing first, and let the heavier abstract task ride whatever lift shows up afterward.

**Examples:**
- Meal prep before studying, not the reverse — cutting and cooking gives immediate visible progress that study doesn't.
- A walk before answering emails.
- Tidying one surface before starting deskwork.

---

## 6. Protect the quiet hour, and let a good start carry — but don't force it

There's usually one window in the day that's naturally the least heavy — often right after waking, before the day fully loads in. Guard that slot for the anchor task, and don't fill it with decisions. And when finishing one thing does leave you lifted, that's a good moment to slide into a second task while capacity is briefly higher — but it's an offer, not an obligation. If the lift doesn't show up, one anchor done is still a complete, real day.

**Examples:**
- Doing the hardest or most-avoided task in the first hour after waking, before anything else gets a chance to derail it.
- After a workout leaves you energized, using that window for a task you'd normally avoid — but only if it's there, never forcing it.
- If the lift doesn't come after the first task, stopping there without treating it as a failure.

---

## Worked example: building a chain

This is the structure already worked out for tomorrow's exercise, useful as a template for building a chain in any other area:

1. **Remove tonight's decision from tomorrow morning.** Bag packed and by the door the night before — zero setup required on waking.
2. **Convert the decision into motion.** Walking toward the gym is the step that turns "should I go?" into "I'm already going" — once the legs are moving, there's no decision left to renegotiate.
3. **Remove the decision at the destination too.** A preset exercise plan means arriving at the gym isn't a fresh choice — it's just the next domino, already decided.

**To build a chain for a new area, ask three questions:**
- What's the smallest physical first move? *(Rule 2)*
- What decisions can be made in advance, so none are left for the moment itself? *(Rules 2 & 3)*
- Where does it end, and is that decided before it starts? *(Rule 3)*

---

*A missed day is a gap, not a failure. The next quiet hour is always the next chance to start.*
